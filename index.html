Part A · Response

Trevor Paglen's Adversarially Evolved Hallucinations (2017) uses generative adversarial networks (GANs) trained on deliberately constructed image corpora, including Monsters of Capitalism. Rather than treating datasets as neutral collections, Paglen exposes how their selection and classification already encode cultural and ideological assumptions. What is missing from these datasets is the historical and social complexity that exceeds their categories.

The latent space reveals the visual possibilities produced by these classifications, generating unfamiliar and disturbing images from learned statistical relationships. At the same time, it conceals the decisions, exclusions, and power structures that determine what the model can represent.

Paglen's strategy can be understood primarily as glitch and critical exposure. His images disrupt familiar visual conventions, making the underlying logic of machine-generated representation perceptible.

The work challenges the assumption that machine vision objectively represents reality. Instead, it suggests that what a machine can generate is conditioned by how the world has already been selected, organized, and described.

Part B · Project

fridge_corpus

Introduction

fridge_corpus begins with 45 photographs of objects from my fridge, each accompanied by a personal definition.

In a single ChatGPT conversation, this collection becomes a description of a person, and then a generated collection of fridge objects.

The project asks what survives these translations, and what the model adds along the way. The original photographs and definitions remain visible alongside the generated material, allowing viewers to examine the relationship between my collection and the person the model constructs from it.

  1. 01

    Collecting

    I photographed 45 objects from my fridge. Together, these images form the original collection, fridge_corpus.

    😊 For privacy, I covered every face in the photographs with an emoji.

    Shown: all 45 original photographs, numbered consistently. → Collection
  2. 02

    Defining

    I wrote a personal definition for each photographed object. These definitions introduce my own language and associations into the collection. They are part of the material the model receives, not captions added after its interpretation.

    Shown: each photograph with its exact personal definition. → Collection
  3. 03

    Inferring a person

    I submitted the photographs and definitions to ChatGPT and asked it to describe the person behind fridge_corpus. The response translates a collection of objects and personal statements into an inferred identity.

    Shown: the exact prompt and complete first response. → Conversation 1
  4. 04

    Reconstructing a fridge

    In the same conversation, I asked ChatGPT to generate new objects for this person to put on their fridge, repeating the request until there were 45 objects. The inferred person became the starting point for a new, generated image collection.

    Shown: the exact prompt and the generated images (). → Conversation 2
Approach and tool

I replaced the source archive with a personal collection and used repeated image-to-text and text-to-image transformations to produce a second collection. I used ChatGPT to interpret the original photographs and definitions and to generate new object images from its description. The inference and generation stages took place in the same conversation.

How the project responds to Paglen

In response to Paglen’s investigation of curated datasets and machine-generated images, I introduce a collection whose personal meanings I can place alongside the machine’s interpretations. Rather than training a model, I use an existing system to move from objects to an inferred person and back to objects. This shifts the emphasis toward the distance between my own definitions and the identity the model constructs from them.

When the model describes the person behind the second fridge, what remains of the person who assembled the first?
