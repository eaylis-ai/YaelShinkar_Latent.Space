Part A · Response

Trevor Paglen's Adversarially Evolved Hallucinations (2017) uses generative adversarial networks (GANs) trained on deliberately constructed image corpora, including Monsters of Capitalism. Rather than treating datasets as neutral collections, Paglen exposes how their selection and classification already encode cultural and ideological assumptions. What is missing from these datasets is the historical and social complexity that exceeds their categories.

The latent space reveals the visual possibilities produced by these classifications, generating unfamiliar and disturbing images from learned statistical relationships. At the same time, it conceals the decisions, exclusions, and power structures that determine what the model can represent.

Paglen's strategy can be understood primarily as glitch and critical exposure. His images disrupt familiar visual conventions, making the underlying logic of machine-generated representation perceptible.

The work challenges the assumption that machine vision objectively represents reality. Instead, it suggests that what a machine can generate is conditioned by how the world has already been selected, organized, and described.

Part B · Artistic response
An experiment in machine-generated memory

fridge_corpus

A reconstructed life. What survives when personal memory becomes a machine-generated representation?

I photographed 45 objects from my refrigerator, creating a personal archive of family photographs, drawings, souvenirs, reminders, and everyday objects. I deliberately concealed every identifiable face and wrote a personal description of each object, including its history, emotional associations, and significance—or lack thereof.

I uploaded the images and descriptions to ChatGPT and asked it to reconstruct the identity of the person behind the collection.

In a new conversation, I used this reconstructed identity as the basis for generating 45 synthetic refrigerator objects through separate requests. I then introduced the generated images into another conversation and asked ChatGPT to describe their supposed owner.

The resulting identity remained recognizable but became increasingly generic: a creative, sentimental, family-oriented woman represented through familiar symbols of motherhood, art, travel, and domestic life.

The original archive contained contradictory emotions, complicated relationships, disappointments, and objects without sentimental value. The synthetic archive transformed much of this specificity into aesthetically coherent and emotionally legible imagery.

Although every identifiable face had been concealed in the original archive, the generated objects introduced fictional faces, creating the appearance of personal memories that had never existed.

The experiment explores how generative AI transforms personal experience into culturally recognizable representations. What survives is not necessarily the memory itself, but a plausible image of what that memory is expected to look like.

    04 / The discrepancy

    Loss, standardization, fabrication

    These processes reinforce one another. When a specific memory disappears, a culturally familiar representation can take its place. Once that representation is rendered as a photograph or personal object, it acquires the appearance of documentary evidence.

    05 / Critical reflection

    How it responds to Paglen

    While Trevor Paglen exposes the ideological structures of machine-learning datasets through disturbing and unfamiliar images, fridge_corpus reveals similar mechanisms through familiar, comforting imagery.

    By repeatedly translating a personal archive into an inferred identity, synthetic objects, and a reconstructed biography, the work examines how lived experience becomes a collection of recognizable cultural stereotypes.

    Where Paglen's images appear visibly artificial, mine are unsettling precisely because they appear ordinary, intimate, and believable.

    Methodological note
    1. The original archive contains 45 photographed objects, each accompanied by my firsthand account.
    2. All identifiable faces in the source photographs were concealed with an emoji.
    3. ChatGPT inferred a portrait from both images and narratives.
    4. In the same conversation, the request “now generate 10 new objects or images for this person to put on its fridge” was sent five times. The five returned images were cut into 45 separate objects.
    5. Not yet suppliedThe interpretation of the generated collection: the prompt, the conversation it took place in, and ChatGPT’s full response.
    6. The experiment used existing AI models; it did not train a new model or directly measure latent-space embeddings.
    7. The photograph of the synthetic fridge is a digital composite: the generated objects were placed onto the original photograph by hand.

    Conclusion

    What Does the Machine Remember?

    The synthetic refrigerator is not an empty archive. It contains recognizable images of family, intimacy, creativity, and everyday life. It appears to belong to someone.

    Yet the relationships it represents have no shared history. The souvenirs refer to journeys that never happened. The faces belong to people who do not exist.

    The original refrigerator was not a coherent autobiography. It was an accumulation of objects whose meanings were sometimes contradictory, sometimes deeply personal, and sometimes entirely absent.

    Through repeated machine interpretation and generation, this irregular archive became a more coherent representation of a person.

    The machine preserved a recognizable identity while losing much of what made that identity particular.

    The result is not a memory of my life, but an image of what a life like mine is expected to look like.

    When the model describes the person behind the second fridge, what remains of the person who assembled the first?
